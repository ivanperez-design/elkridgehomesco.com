What a Steamboat Remodel Takes — Before a Wall Comes Down

Building well at 6,695 feet is different from building in Denver. This page lays out what a large remodel or addition in Steamboat actually involves: the mountain conditions, a realistic timeline, the decisions you'll make and when, what your proposal lists line by line, and what older homes tend to hide. Read it before you sign with anyone.

The mountain part

Why a Steamboat remodel is different

Five conditions shape every project here. We design around them from the first walkthrough.

Altitude. Combustion appliances lose rated capacity at 6,695 feet, and cure times for adhesives, paint and concrete change. Heavy physical work simply takes longer up here, and the schedule should say so.
Snow load. Routt County snow loads are set per parcel. Roofs, decks, additions and large new openings are engineered to the load at your address, not copied from a Front Range plan.
Wildfire zones. Much of Steamboat sits in the wildland-urban interface. On mapped parcels, Class A roofing and ignition-resistant siding are part of the scope, not an upgrade.
Freeze-thaw and frost. Materials expand and contract hard here, and footings go down 48 inches. Details that hold in milder climates fail fast if they're specified wrong.
Dry mountain air. Indoor humidity drops very low in winter. Hardwood, trim and cabinetry are acclimated on site before install so they don't move after we leave.
Remote delivery. Mountain delivery adds roughly 3 to 7 days to material lead times, and trades book 3 to 4 weeks out from June to September. Long-lead items get ordered early for that reason.
The honest calendar

A realistic timeline

Quality takes longer than most people expect. These are typical ranges; your written schedule will be specific to your project.

PhaseTypical duration
Pre-construction: scope, drawings, selections2–4 weeks
Permitting2–6 weeks
Long-lead materials (windows, cabinets, custom millwork)6–14 weeks
Demo to closeout, medium remodel10–18 weeks
Demo to closeout, large remodel or addition14–28 weeks
Winter workadd 20–30%

Why permits take weeks: structural work such as additions, snow-load engineering and foundations often needs engineer-stamped drawings, and the county review queue is real. We submit clean packages; we don't control the queue.

Why winter adds time: cold-weather concrete needs protection, paint won't cure below about 50°F, drywall mud needs supplemental heat, and snow eats staging space. We plan for it rather than pretend it isn't there.

What your proposal lists

Every cost, line by line

A one-number bid hides where the money goes. Your proposal separates the two kinds of line items, so you can see every cost before anything starts.

Fixed lines

Work we can define exactly: demolition and haul-off, framing and structural work, rough-in, insulation, drywall, trim labor, protection of your home, permits and engineering coordination. Each is written as its own line.

Allowances

Categories where the choice is yours: cabinets, countertops, tile, plumbing and lighting fixtures, hardware, appliances and finish level. Each allowance is stated in writing. Go over and the difference is shown before it's ordered; come in under and you keep the savings.

Changes

Every change in scope is written up and priced, and you sign it before the work happens. No verbal change orders, no surprise invoice at the end.

Payments

Draws are tied to milestones you can see and inspect, such as framing complete or rough-in passed, and the schedule is written into your contract. Final payment comes after your walkthrough and sign-off.

Behind the walls

Surprises that shouldn't be surprises

Remodels uncover what the original construction hid. We look for these at the walkthrough and test before demo. Anything we find is documented with photos, priced in writing and approved by you before work continues.

Asbestos and lead paint. Homes built before 1980 get tested before any demolition.
Old wiring and piping. Knob-and-tube wiring and galvanized supply lines show up in older homes and are best replaced while walls are open.
Rot where snow sat. Sill plates and rim boards under decks and drifts are checked at demo.
Settled log and post-and-beam walls. Common in older Steamboat homes; new work is scribed to them.
Engineering for additions. New loads and large openings usually need a structural engineer's stamp.
Wildfire-code gaps. Existing roofing or siding that isn't up to current standards can change the scope on mapped parcels.
During the build

What it's like while we work

You always know where it stands

Jobsite photos and a short written recap every week: what got done, what's next, and any decision we need from you. Messages returned the same business day. Every verbal decision gets confirmed in writing the same day.

Permitted and inspected

We permit the work. Footings, framing, rough mechanical, electrical and plumbing, and insulation are each inspected before they're covered up, and we don't move past an inspection until it passes.

Living in the house

Dust is contained with sealed work zones and filtration. Working hours, parking, dumpster placement and pets are agreed before day one. For whole-home or demo-heavy work, a short move-out is often easier, and we'll tell you honestly if it is.

Bring your architect, or don't

Kitchen and bath work usually needs no architect. Additions, structural changes and whole-home remodels often do. We'll tell you which side your project falls on, and work alongside an architect you already have.
