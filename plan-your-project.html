Path 1 · Custom Builds

Planning a new home

For a ground-up build, the most useful things to bring are photos of the lot, your land and parcel status, where your plans and financing stand, and a budget range — the land and the money matter as much as the design. Open the checklist for the full list.

Custom build planning checklist
  • Photos — of the lot from a few angles, the access road or driveway, the view you're building toward, and any existing structures or trees that stay or go.
  • Lot & parcel readiness — the address or parcel number, whether you own the land yet, and what's already on it: power, water or well, septic or sewer, and existing access. Tell us if it's raw or already platted.
  • Plans & architect status — do you have drawings, a floor plan sketch, or an architect engaged, or are you starting from scratch? Even a square-footage target and bedroom and bath counts help us frame the conversation.
  • Financing — whether you're paying cash, pre-approved for a construction loan, or still exploring. We don't need numbers, just where you are, so we plan a realistic draw schedule.
  • Style examples — save photos of homes, rooms, rooflines, and materials you're drawn to. A mountain-modern exterior, a warm timber great room, a standing-seam roof — they tell us your taste in seconds.
  • A budget range — even a rough one. On a custom build it shapes everything from footprint to finish level, and it lets us be honest with you early instead of late.
  • Timeline questions — when you'd like to break ground, when you'd like to move in, and any season, lease end, or life event you're building toward. Mountain weather and permitting both factor in.
  • Material preferences — leanings on siding, roofing, windows, flooring, and how the home should feel inside. We'll refine all of it, but a starting point speeds the early design.
  • Parcel notes — if the lot is in a mapped wildfire zone, an HOA with design review, or a steep or constrained site, tell us up front so we scope it right.

Path 2 · Remodels

Reworking an existing home

For a remodel, the two most useful things are photos of what's there and a clear sense of what's bothering you — a remodel lives or dies on the existing conditions. Open the checklist for the rest.

Remodel planning checklist
  • Photos — wide shots of each space you want to change, plus close-ups of anything that's worn, dated, or not working. Shots of adjoining rooms help too, since remodels rarely stop at one doorway.
  • Measurements that help — rough room dimensions and ceiling heights, or a quick floor-plan sketch with approximate sizes. A tape measure and a phone note are plenty to start.
  • Style examples — save a handful of rooms, finishes, and layouts you love. Even pins that contradict each other are useful — they show us the range you're choosing within.
  • A budget range — even a rough one. It lets us steer scope and material grade to fit, and to tell you early what's realistic in the space you have.
  • Timeline questions — when you'd like to start, whether you'll be living in the home during the work, and any event or season you're working toward.
  • Material preferences — leanings on cabinetry, countertops, flooring, tile, and fixtures. A starting point speeds selections; we'll refine the rest together.
  • Current problems — what's actually driving the project: a layout that fights you, not enough storage, dated finishes, a leak, a draft, or a room that's never warm. These shape the scope more than the wish list does.
  • The home's age — roughly when it was built, and whether it's been remodeled before. Older homes can hide surprises behind the walls, and we plan for that up front.

Path 3 · Interiors

Kitchens, baths & everything inside

For interior work — kitchen, bath, basement, closets, flooring, or a whole-home refresh — the most useful thing is being specific about what you want to keep and what you want gone. Open the checklist for what to gather.

Interior planning checklist
  • Photos — each room from the corners so we see the whole space, plus close-ups of cabinets, counters, tile, fixtures, and flooring. For a bath, shot the shower, vanity, and any water damage; for a kitchen, the run of cabinets and the appliance layout.
  • Measurements that help — room dimensions, ceiling height, and for kitchens and baths the rough sizes of counters, the island, or the shower. A sketch with numbers is ideal; a tape and a phone note works fine.
  • Style examples — save finished kitchens, baths, and rooms you love: cabinet color, counter material, tile pattern, flooring tone, fixture finish. They translate your taste faster than words.
  • A budget range — even a rough one. Interior finishes span a wide range, so a range lets us match cabinetry, counters, and tile to what you have in mind.
  • Timeline questions — your ideal start, whether you'll live in the home during the work, and how you'll manage a kitchen or main bath being out of service.
  • Material preferences — leanings on cabinetry style, countertop material, flooring, tile, and fixtures. See the materials we build with for a sense of the range, then tell us where your taste lands.
  • Current problems — what isn't working: too little storage, a cramped layout, a dated finish, poor lighting, a slow drain, a soft floor, or a basement that's cold or damp. The frustration is the brief.

Path 4 · Exteriors

Siding, windows, doors & the building envelope

For exterior work, photos of each face of the home plus the spots where things have failed give us the fastest read on what you're dealing with. Open the checklist for the rest. (For roofing and decks specifically, see the dedicated path below.)

Exterior planning checklist
  • Photos — wide shots of each elevation, plus close-ups of failing siding, peeling stain, rotted trim, drafty or fogged windows, and a door that won't seal or swing true.
  • Measurements that help — rough wall square footage, or a count of windows and doors with approximate sizes. A pace-count along each wall is plenty to start.
  • Style examples — save exteriors you love: a siding color and profile, a stain tone, a window style. They tell us your taste in seconds.
  • A budget range — even a rough one. It lets us steer material grade and scope to fit instead of guessing.
  • Timeline questions — when you'd like to start, any season you're working toward, and whether the home is occupied or remote during the work. Exterior work is weather-dependent up here, so the calendar matters.
  • Material preferences — fiber-cement vs. wood siding, stain opacity, window line and operation. Even a leaning helps. See our exterior remodels for the options.
  • Current problems — drafts, leaks, water pooling at the foundation, ice at the eaves, or cold rooms. These shape the scope more than anything.
  • Parcel notes — if you're in a mapped wildfire zone or an HOA with exterior rules, tell us up front so we scope ignition-resistant materials and approvals correctly the first time.

Path 5 · Soundproofing

Quieting a room or a shared wall

The single most useful thing to know about soundproofing is that it's far easier and cheaper while the walls are already open — so if a remodel is in the picture, this is the moment. The right fix depends entirely on where the noise comes from and how it travels; open the checklist for what helps us diagnose it.

Soundproofing planning checklist
  • Which rooms — the room you want quiet (a bedroom, office, nursery, theater, or studio) and the room or space on the other side. Photos of both, and of the shared wall, floor, or ceiling, help us picture the assembly.
  • The noise source — what you're actually hearing: voices and TV through a wall, footsteps from above, plumbing, road or street noise, an HVAC unit, or a shared-wall neighbor. Is it constant or occasional, low rumble or sharp and high? The description points us at the right fix.
  • Is a remodel already planned? — soundproofing is dramatically easier while a wall, floor, or ceiling is already open. Tell us if this rides along with other work, because the timing changes the approach and the cost.
  • Measurements that help — rough dimensions of the wall, floor, or ceiling involved, and what's on the other side (another living space, a garage, mechanical room, or the outdoors).
  • Your goal — total quiet for a studio is a different project than just taking the edge off a noisy neighbor. Tell us how quiet is quiet enough, since that drives how far the assembly needs to go.
  • A budget range and timeline — even rough. And note any flanking paths you already know about — a shared duct, a gap under a door, an outlet box backed up to the neighbor — since sound takes every path it can find.

Path 6 · Roofing, Siding & Decks

The parts that take the weather

For the parts that take mountain winters — snow, ice, freeze-thaw, altitude UV — the most useful thing is a little history on each one's age and what's gone wrong, which tells us whether you're looking at a repair or a rebuild. Open the checklist for the details.

Roofing, siding & deck planning checklist
  • Photos — the roof from the ground on each side, the deck from above and underneath (posts, beams, and ledger), and any siding around them. Close-ups of curling shingles, rust, soft deck boards, loose railings, or water stains tell the story fast.
  • Roof age — roughly how old the roof is, what it's made of (asphalt, metal, other), and whether it's ever been replaced or layered over. Age and material set the whole conversation.
  • Leak & ice-dam history — any interior staining, drips, attic moisture, or ice building up at the eaves in winter. Where and when it shows up tells us where the assembly is failing.
  • Deck condition — how old it is, the decking material, and whether boards are soft, fasteners are backing out, the railing is loose, or it moves when you walk it. A wobble or a soft board is a safety flag worth noting.
  • Measurements that help — rough roof or deck square footage and the deck footprint. A pace-count of length and width is plenty to start.
  • Style & material preferences — asphalt vs. standing-seam metal, composite vs. wood decking, and railing type (cable, metal, glass, or wood). Even a leaning helps. See our roofing, siding, and decks pages for the options.
  • Budget range & timeline — even rough, plus any season you're working toward. Roofing and exterior work is weather-dependent, so timing matters.
  • WUI & HOA notes — if your parcel is in a mapped wildland-urban-interface zone, Class A roofing and ignition-resistant siding may be required, and an HOA may review materials. Tell us up front so we scope it right the first time.

The Steamboat building-science FAQ

Straight answers before you reach out

The honest answers to what people actually ask us about building and remodeling at 6,700 feet — the market and the land, the process and the budget, and the team. Every Steamboat-specific number below is something we confirm for your parcel before we scope it.

Market & land — building in the Yampa Valley

What's different about building at 6,700 feet in Steamboat?
A home in the Yampa Valley answers to snow, fire, cold, and distance in ways a Front Range or coastal build never sees, so we resolve those before a scope is written:
  • Roofs, decks, and frames sized to the ground snow load for your parcel — Routt County is a case-study snow zone with no single number, so we verify it via Routt County GIS.
  • Footings to a 48-inch frost depth countywide.
  • A Climate Zone 7B envelope — continuous exterior insulation and triple-pane glazing — since at altitude the envelope sets comfort and operating cost for the life of the home.
  • Remote-delivery lead times — at this distance from suppliers, material can add roughly 3 to 7 days to a schedule.
My lot is in a wildfire (WUI) zone — what does that change?
On a mapped wildland-urban-interface parcel we build ignition-resistant assemblies and confirm exactly what your parcel requires before scoping it. Fire resistance here is a system, not a single product:
  • A Class A roof assembly, ignition-resistant siding, and ember-resistant detailing at vents and edges.
  • Deck-to-wall detailing and defensible space, which the rest of the assembly depends on to do its job.
  • We will tell you plainly what an assembly can and cannot do — we build the system the code and science call for and install it correctly, and we do not promise a home will survive a fire.
What energy and window standards apply to a Steamboat home?
Climate Zone 7B is one of the coldest in Colorado, so we build a tight, well-insulated envelope and hold window U-factors at or below 0.30 for the zone.
  • Continuous exterior insulation and triple-pane glazing are our default here, not an upgrade.
  • We confirm the currently adopted energy code and the U-factor requirement in effect when your windows are ordered.
Do I need to own the land before I talk to you?
No — talk to us at any stage, including while you're still evaluating a lot, because the parcel itself drives feasibility. The earlier we look at it, the fewer surprises later:
  • Access, power, water or well, and septic or sewer status all shape what a build will take.
  • Snow load, the WUI map, and any HOA design review are parcel-specific — knowing the lot lets us flag them before you commit.
What if it turns out we're not the right fit for your project?
We'll tell you honestly and, where we can, point you toward someone better suited. We would rather be straight with you early than take on work we can't do right — a quick conversation usually tells both of us whether there's a fit.

Process, timeline & budget

Do I need to have all of this ready before I contact you?
No. None of it is required — call or text whenever you're ready and we'll take it from there. This page is just a head start: the more of it you have on hand, the faster and tighter your written proposal comes back.
What's the single most useful thing I can send first?
Photos. Wide shots of the space or elevation plus close-ups of any problem area tell us more in a minute than a long description. Send them along with a rough budget range and your timeline and we can usually tell you quickly whether we're the right fit.
Should I share a budget even if I'm not sure?
Yes — even a rough range helps. It lets us steer material grade and scope to fit what you have in mind instead of guessing high or low. We hold the price in a written, line-item scope, and it changes only by a change order you approve.
How much will my project cost?
Every project is priced from its own scope, so we don't publish numbers that could mislead you — the honest answer comes from a walkthrough and a written, line-item proposal. What moves the price up or down:
  • Scope and size, finish level and material grade, and how much of the existing structure stays or goes.
  • Parcel factors — snow load, WUI requirements, site access, and the envelope the climate zone calls for.
  • Lead times and seasonality, since remote delivery and a short building season both affect what a schedule costs.
The fastest way to a real number is to request a consultation — we return a written proposal within 48 hours of your walkthrough.
How long does a project take here?
Timelines run from a few weeks for a focused interior to many months for a ground-up build, and in the mountains the calendar matters more than most places. What drives it:
  • Permitting and design review, which vary by jurisdiction and HOA.
  • Remote-delivery lead times that can add several days to material-dependent steps.
  • A short, weather-dependent building season, especially for roofing, siding, and decks.
  • Sub availability — in peak season, June through September, trades book up weeks ahead, so we schedule early.
When is the best time to start, given the season?
It depends on the work: interior projects run year-round, while exterior work is weather-dependent and best planned around the building season. Because peak-season subs book up weeks ahead, the earlier we plan, the more the calendar works in your favor.
How do I send photos of my project?
Text them to 970-393-6239 or email info@elkridgeinteriors.com. Wide shots plus close-ups of any problem area are ideal, and a quick note on what's bothering you about the space helps us scope it right the first time.

The team & how we work

My home was built before 1980 — is that a problem?
It's not a dealbreaker, but it does mean we test before we demolish, because finishes and materials from before 1980 can contain asbestos or lead.
  • We test suspect materials before any demo so a remodel never disturbs something hazardous without a plan to handle it safely.
  • Older homes can also hide surprises behind the walls — we plan for that in the scope rather than letting it become a mid-job change order.
Will I work directly with the owner, or get handed off?
You work with the principal of the company on every project — there's no call center and no handoff to a salesperson who disappears after the contract. Both decision-makers are on every project message, so no detail lands with only one of you.
How will I know what's happening during the build?
You see every working day: a photo and a status update each day, plus a short written recap each week. The price lives in a written, line-item scope that changes only by a change order you approve, so there are no surprises on the invoice.
Already have plans or an architect?
Bring them — we'll estimate your drawings and build them right, working alongside your architect without making you start over. If you're starting from a blank lot or a single room with no drawings yet, we help you shape the scope and develop the plan from there.
What does your warranty cover?
We put a written workmanship warranty on every project, with a term longer than the one-year norm common in the trade. The specific term and what it covers are spelled out in your written agreement.
