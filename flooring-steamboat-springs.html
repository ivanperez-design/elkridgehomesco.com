A new floor changes a home more than almost any other single project — and most of what makes it last happens before the first plank or tile goes down. The material you see is only as good as the subfloor, the moisture check, and the prep underneath it. This page walks you through the decisions that shape the result — hardwood versus tile versus luxury vinyl, what the prep really involves, and the mountain-specific factors that flatland installers never deal with — so you know enough to make confident calls without becoming a flooring pro yourself.

Flooring runs through nearly every interior remodel we take on — and it stands on its own just as well.

The educational core

The decisions that shape a floor — in plain English.

You don't need to master all of these. You just need to know they exist and which ones matter for your home. We handle the prep and the install; this is so you can steer the decisions.

Hardwood

The classic, and the one with the most nuance up here. Solid hardwood is timeless but moves with humidity, and our dry mountain air can make it cup or gap if it isn't handled right; engineered hardwood is more stable across temperature and moisture swings, which matters a lot at altitude and over in-floor heat. What matters: the wood is acclimated to your home before it's installed, and the right type is chosen for your subfloor, your climate, and whether you have radiant heat.

Tile

The most durable and water-tolerant choice, and the warmest, most efficient floor there is over radiant heat. Tile is the right call for entries, mudrooms, baths, and anywhere snowmelt and water show up. What matters: tile is only as flat and crack-free as the substrate under it, so the subfloor has to be stiff, level, and prepped with the right underlayment before a single tile is set.

Luxury vinyl plank (LVP)

The practical workhorse — waterproof at the plank, durable, warmer underfoot than tile on its own, and forgiving of the moisture and traffic a mountain home throws at it. Quality rigid-core LVP looks convincingly like wood and handles entries and high-use areas well. What matters: even waterproof flooring needs a properly prepped, flat subfloor and a moisture-checked slab — and radiant-heat installs follow the manufacturer's temperature limits so the floor performs as it should.

Subfloor prep

The invisible step that decides everything. Before any flooring goes down, the subfloor has to be sound, level, and flat — fastened so it doesn't squeak, ground or filled where it's high or low, and repaired where it's soft. What matters: skipping or rushing prep is the single most common reason floors fail early, so we level and prep first, every time, no matter what goes on top.

Moisture testing

Especially over concrete slabs and in below-grade spaces, moisture is what wrecks a floor — and you can't see it without testing for it. What matters: we test the slab's moisture before installing, because the wrong number means the wrong floor or the wrong adhesive, and a floor installed over a wet slab can fail no matter how good the material is.

Radiant-heat compatibility

Many Steamboat homes have in-floor heat, and not every floor plays nicely with it. Tile is the best performer over radiant; engineered wood is the right wood choice; LVP works when the manufacturer's temperature limits are respected; solid hardwood is the riskiest over heat. What matters: the flooring is matched to your radiant system and installed to the temperature specs, so the heat works and the floor doesn't cup, gap, or void its warranty.

Transitions & thresholds

The small detail that reads as "well built" — where one floor meets another, where the floor meets a door, and especially the transition at an exterior entry where snowmelt arrives. What matters: clean, level transitions and the right threshold at the door, so there are no trip edges, no gaps, and water at the entry has somewhere to go instead of into the floor.

What it costs

What actually drives the cost of flooring — so you can steer it.

Two floors the same size can land far apart on material, prep, and what's underneath. We won't quote a number sight-unseen, but we'll be straight about what moves it, so you steer the budget before we start, not discover it after.

Material choice — LVP, tile, engineered wood, and solid hardwood span a wide range per square foot, and within each there's a budget-to-premium spread.
Subfloor condition — a sound, flat subfloor installs cleanly; one that needs leveling, re-fastening, or repair adds labor before the floor even starts.
Demo and disposal — tearing out and hauling old flooring, especially tile or glued-down material, adds work; and pre-1980 homes may need testing before demo.
Radiant heat — installing over or alongside in-floor heat means matching the right material and following temperature specs, which shapes both the choice and the method.
Tile complexity and pattern — large-format tile, intricate layouts, and detailed transitions take more prep and labor than a straightforward plank floor.

A useful rule of thumb: LVP is the practical, cost-efficient choice, while hardwood and premium tile are lasting investments in how the home looks and lives. Every floor is priced to your home, your material, the prep it needs, and a written scope — never a number pulled from the air.

We talk budget honestly at the walkthrough — before you spend on selections — so you know what's realistic for your home and your subfloor.
The written scope spells out material, the prep the subfloor needs, and any radiant-heat work as clear line items, so you can see exactly where the money goes and adjust before we start.
Every change is priced in writing and approved by you before it happens — the budget moves only when you decide it does.

Built for 6,700 feet

A mountain floor deals with things a flatland floor never does.

We build for where your home actually sits — dry air, in-floor heat, snowmelt at the door, and owners who aren't always in town.

Wood movement in dry, high-altitude air

Our climate is dry, and wood floors respond to it — install without acclimating the wood to your home first and you risk cupping, gapping, and a floor that telegraphs every season. We let the material acclimate on site before it goes down, and we steer you toward engineered hardwood where stability matters most, so the floor stays flat year-round.

Radiant-heat-compatible flooring, matched on purpose

Many Steamboat homes run on in-floor heat, and the floor has to be chosen for it — tile performs best, engineered wood is the right wood, LVP works within its temperature limits, and solid hardwood is the riskiest. We match the material to your radiant system and install to the temperature specs, so the heat works and the floor lasts.

Moisture from snowmelt at entries

Mudrooms, entries, and the strip just inside the door take the brunt of snowmelt and wet boots. We put water-tolerant flooring — tile or quality LVP — where the water shows up, with the right threshold and transition at the door, so melt has somewhere to go instead of soaking into the floor.

Older homes get tested before demo

Homes built before 1980 can hide asbestos in old flooring and adhesives, so we test before anything comes up — no surprises mid-demo, no exposure to your family. It's a quiet step most crews skip and the right one to take.

Flooring questions

Steamboat flooring — common questions.

Hardwood, tile, or LVP — which is right for my home?
It depends on the room, how it's used, and whether you have radiant heat — not on which sounds best. Hardwood brings warmth and resale value but moves with our dry air, so engineered is often the smarter pick up here. Tile is the most durable and the best over in-floor heat, ideal for entries and wet areas. LVP is the practical, waterproof workhorse for everyday life. We walk your rooms at the walkthrough and match the material to how you actually live, then put it in your written scope.
Can you install flooring over my in-floor radiant heat?
Yes — and matching the floor to the heat is one of the things we're careful about. Tile performs best over radiant, engineered wood is the right wood choice, and LVP works as long as we follow the manufacturer's temperature limits. Solid hardwood is the riskiest over heat. We install to the temperature specs so the heat works and the floor doesn't cup, gap, or void its warranty.
Why does the subfloor matter so much?
Because the floor you see is only as good as what's under it. A subfloor that isn't sound, level, and flat leads to squeaks, cracked tile, gaps, and a floor that fails early — it's the single most common reason a new floor goes wrong. We make the subfloor right and moisture-test the slab where it matters before any material goes down, every time.
Do you acclimate wood flooring before installing it?
Yes, and at altitude it matters more than most places. Our dry mountain air can make wood cup or gap if it's installed straight off the truck, so we let the material acclimate to your home's conditions first, and we steer you toward engineered hardwood where stability matters most — so the floor stays flat through the seasons.
What flooring holds up best at a snowy mountain entry?
Tile and quality LVP, because they shrug off snowmelt and wet boots where hardwood would suffer. We put water-tolerant flooring where the water shows up — mudrooms, entries, the strip just inside the door — with the right threshold and transition so the melt has somewhere to go instead of soaking into the floor.
Can you plan flooring for a second home while I'm out of town?
Yes. Remote and second-home owners do selections with us by video and photo, with every decision documented, so you never have to fly in to pick a floor. Your home is protected and broom-clean at the end of every working day.
What does the 2-year workmanship warranty cover?
Our written workmanship warranty covers the quality of our installation — it's above the 1-year norm most local builders offer. We walk the finished floor with you, resolve any open items at the final walkthrough, and the warranty starts that day. Manufacturer warranties on the flooring material are separate and carry their own terms, which we make sure you have.
