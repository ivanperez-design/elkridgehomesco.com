A kitchen is the most ambitious room most people remodel, and the one with the most moving parts — cabinetry, stone, plumbing, electrical, tile, flooring, and appliance logistics, all on one tight schedule in one tight space. This page walks you through the decisions that actually shape the result, what drives the cost, and how we keep the whole thing from becoming a surprise. The educational core

The eight decisions that shape a kitchen — in plain English.

You don't need to master all of these. You just need to know they exist and which ones matter for your home. We handle the sequencing; this is so you can steer the decisions.

Layout & flow

How the room works when you're actually cooking in it — the path between sink, range, and refrigerator, where two people pass, where the trash and the prep zone live. Getting the flow right is the single biggest quality-of-life decision in the room, and it's the one that's hardest to change once cabinets are set.

What matters: solve how you use the kitchen before you pick a single finish.

Cabinetry

Usually the largest line in the budget and the backbone of the look. The real choice is stock vs. semi-custom vs. fully custom — a tradeoff between cost, lead time, and how precisely it fits your space and ceiling height.

What matters: cabinets set level and plumb so doors and drawers line up and stay aligned for twenty years, not twenty months.

Countertops

Quartz, granite, natural stone, solid-surface, butcher block, or laminate — a wide span in cost and care. The detail most people miss: the counter has to be templated to your actual installed cabinets, not to a drawing, so seams, overhangs, and the sink cutout land where they should.

What matters: template after the cabinets are in, every time.

Appliances & venting

Builder-grade to professional-series is a wide swing on its own, and panel-ready and built-in units add cabinetry and labor. Venting is the part people forget: a real range hood has to move air outside, and at altitude, gas appliances and combustion air deserve a second look.

What matters: confirm appliance specs and delivery dates early, because a late range can strand a finished kitchen.

Lighting & electrical

A modern kitchen needs more circuits and better light than an old one — recessed, undercabinet, and island lighting, plus dedicated circuits for the appliances.

What matters: it gets planned and rough-wired before cabinets hang, not fished in late after the boxes are up.

Plumbing

Keeping the sink and dishwasher where they are is the budget-friendly path; moving them, adding an island sink, or running a pot filler means new rough-in.

What matters: every move is roughed in before cabinets go up, so nothing gets opened back up after the finish work is done.

Flooring

Hardwood, tile, or luxury vinyl — and the floor is only as good as the prep under it.

What matters: the subfloor is leveled and moisture-checked first, so the floor lies flat and stays that way under the new kitchen.

Finishes

Backsplash and accent tile, hardware, paint, and the trim that ties it together — the layer you see and touch every day.

What matters: tile laid out so the cuts land where they should and grouted clean; the small details are what read as "well built."

What it costs

What actually drives the cost of a kitchen — so you can steer it.

A kitchen is one of the widest-ranging projects in a home — two kitchens the same size can land thousands apart on a handful of choices. We won't quote a number sight-unseen, but we'll be straight about what moves it, so you steer the budget before demo, not discover it after.

The five biggest budget levers

  1. 01

    Cabinet grade

    Stock vs. semi-custom vs. fully custom is the single biggest lever. More boxes, taller runs, specialty interiors, and custom door styles add up.

  2. 02

    Countertop material

    Laminate to high-end natural stone spans a wide range per square foot, and the slab, edge profile, and seams factor in.

  3. 03

    The appliance package

    Builder-grade vs. professional-series can swing the budget on its own; panel-ready and built-in units add cabinetry and labor.

  4. 04

    Moving plumbing and electrical

    Keeping fixtures where they are is the budget-friendly path; relocating a sink, adding a pot filler, or moving a range means more rough-in.

  5. 05

    Structural and layout changes

    Removing a wall, changing the footprint, or reworking the work triangle adds engineering, framing, and the trades that follow.

How we keep you in control of the budget

We talk budget honestly at the walkthrough — before you spend on selections — so you know what's realistic for your home.
The written scope spells out cabinet grade, counter material, and the appliance package as clear line items, so you can see exactly where the money goes and adjust before demo.
Every change is priced in writing and approved by you before it happens — the budget moves only when you decide it does.

Built for 6,700 feet

A Steamboat kitchen has factors a flatland kitchen never deals with.

We build for where your home actually sits — altitude, in-floor heat, remote delivery, and owners who aren't always in town.

Venting and combustion at altitude.

A real range hood has to vent outside, and at 6,700 feet, gas appliances burn with less oxygen — so the venting and combustion-air details deserve a closer look than they get at sea level. We plan the hood, ductwork, and make-up air so the kitchen clears smoke and steam the way it should.

Radiant and in-floor heat, coordinated — not cut into later.

Many Steamboat kitchens sit over in-floor heat. We coordinate flooring, cabinet runs, and any plumbing moves around the radiant system so it keeps working and nothing gets drilled or sliced where it shouldn't be.

Older homes get tested before demo.

Homes built before 1980 can hide asbestos or lead, so we test before anything comes off a wall — no surprises mid-demo, no exposure to your family. It's a quiet step most crews skip and the right one to take.

Material lead times run long up here.

Remote mountain delivery adds roughly 3–7 days to most material lead times, and custom cabinetry and stone can run weeks regardless. We confirm lead times before we set the schedule and order long-lead items early, so the calendar reflects reality instead of hope.

Get ready to talk

Five questions to sit with before your walkthrough.

You don't need answers to all of these — they're just the things worth thinking about. Come with a sense of them and your walkthrough goes further, faster.

  1. 01

    How do you actually cook and gather here?

    One cook or two, quick weeknight meals or big holiday spreads, kids doing homework at the island — the way you use the kitchen should drive the layout before any finish gets picked.

  2. 02

    What frustrates you most about the kitchen now?

    Not enough counter, an awkward work triangle, too little storage, bad light — naming the real pain points tells us what the remodel has to solve.

  3. 03

    Are you keeping the layout, or do you want to move things?

    Staying put is the budget-friendly path; moving the sink, range, or a wall opens up the room and the budget both. Knowing your appetite for change up front shapes everything.

  4. 04

    What's your timeline relative to ski season and the holidays?

    Cabinetry and stone lead times, plus remote mountain delivery, mean the calendar starts weeks before demo. If you want the kitchen done by a certain date, we work backward from it.

  5. 05

    Where do you want to spend, and where are you fine being practical?

    Most people splurge on one or two things — the range, the stone, custom cabinets — and stay sensible on the rest. Knowing your priorities lets us put the budget where it matters to you.

Kitchen remodel questions

Steamboat kitchen remodels — common questions.

How long does a kitchen remodel take?
Most full kitchen remodels run several weeks of active work, but the honest answer depends on cabinetry and appliance lead times more than the build itself — custom cabinets and stone can take weeks to arrive, and remote mountain delivery adds roughly 3–7 days on top. We confirm lead times before we set your schedule, order long-lead items early, and give you a realistic timeline in your written scope — not an optimistic one.
Can we stay in the home during the remodel?
Usually, yes — most homeowners stay. A kitchen remodel means living without a working kitchen for part of the project, so we talk through a temporary setup (a microwave-and-sink "kitchenette" elsewhere in the home) and sequence the work to keep the rest of your house clean and usable. Your home is protected and broom-clean at the end of every working day.
Do you help with selections and design?
Yes. We help you choose cabinetry, countertops, tile, and finishes that fit your home, your budget, and how a mountain kitchen actually gets used — and we coordinate with your designer if you have one. Remote and second-home owners do selections with us by video and photo, with every decision documented, so you never have to fly in to pick a counter.
Custom or semi-custom cabinets — which is right for my kitchen?
Both have a place. Semi-custom gives you a wide range of sizes, finishes, and styles at a more controlled cost and a shorter lead time. Fully custom is the path when your layout is unusual, your ceilings are tall, or you want a specific door style or built-in that stock sizing can't hit. We walk you through the tradeoff on cost, lead time, and fit at the walkthrough, and the choice goes in your written scope before anything is ordered.
What countertop options do you install?
Quartz, granite, natural stone, solid-surface, butcher block, and laminate — chosen for how the kitchen is used and the budget you're working with. Whatever you pick, we template the counters to your actual installed cabinets, not to a drawing, so the seams, overhangs, and sink cutout land right the first time.
How do appliance lead times affect the schedule?
They're often the real bottleneck — more than the construction. A back-ordered range or a panel-ready refrigerator can hold up an otherwise finished kitchen, so we confirm your appliance specs and ship dates up front and sequence the build around your delivery dates. That keeps a late appliance from stranding the whole project, and keeps your timeline honest.
Do you move plumbing and electrical for a new layout?
Yes — relocating a sink, adding an island with a prep sink or a pot filler, moving the range, or adding the circuits a modern kitchen needs. The key is that we plan and rough all of it in before a single cabinet box hangs, so nothing gets opened back up after the finish work is done.
What does the 2-year workmanship warranty cover?
Our written workmanship warranty covers the quality of our installation — it's above the 1-year norm most local builders offer. We walk the finished kitchen with you, resolve any open items at the final walkthrough, and the warranty starts that day. Manufacturer warranties on cabinets, appliances, and countertops are separate and carry their own terms, which we make sure you have.
